There's a part of success nobody talks about much, and it's that sustaining something over a long time is hard. There are days when you simply don't feel like it, because results usually take longer than you estimate. You publish something that took you months, for example, and it's not unusual for about three people to see it _(one of them a friend or a relative 😅)_. And then the activity that used to excite you starts to feel like an obligation.

In the world of content creation this happens more than we think. You've surely seen creators you followed who, from one moment to the next, stopped posting and turned into plain ghosts.

People with good ideas, talent and knowledge who disappeared. Because what nobody tells you is that sustaining something to please others is utterly exhausting.

Plenty of people will hand you the secret formula: post every day, use this hook, jump on this “trend”, hack the algorithm, measure this and repeat. All of that helps, of course. But for internal motivation there's no formula and no prescription.

It's like going to the gym: there's no point in buying the protein, the clothes, the membership or the watch that measures every drop of sweat if you hate every second of the process. Sooner or later you're going to quit.

It's the same with content creation. If you don't find some satisfaction in what you do, everything costs more and, little by little, it drains your energy and that magical thing many people call motivation.

I'm not saying this from a place of superiority, because I've fallen into that trap too. I started out of curiosity and the urge to share what I'd learned, but little by little external validation creeps in. You start checking the likes, the views, the metrics. The idea settles in that if the algorithm isn't showing it, maybe it's because you should be doing something else.

Before you know it, you're no longer creating from your original purpose but from anxiety, and that's where everything starts to fall apart.

That's why, every now and then, I need to remind myself what that original purpose was, and that it's important to make things for myself first, for yourself.

This doesn't mean ignoring the audience completely. It's also important to listen to feedback, but not all feedback, however good or bad it is, is going to be completely useful and, worse still, it can't be our guide for validation.

The reason is that this validation is very unstable. Sometimes it comes, sometimes it doesn't or it comes too late. There has to be something beyond it that motivates us to keep going.

When you make something for yourself, the worst that can happen is that it only benefits you, and that alone should be reason enough to do it. That it's useful to others should be a consequence of good work, or of how common the problem you're solving is.

When you do it for yourself, there's no loss.

When I make a tutorial, for example, it's usually to organize my ideas, or a stream, because the pressure of being watched motivates me to stay consistent while learning a new topic. Side projects almost always come from the need to solve a problem that affects me and that very likely affects others too.

Thanks to that initial intention of learning something, understanding a concept better or building something I'd like to find, it's possible to sustain the habit of sharing and creating content. The exhaustion, the frustration and the doubts are still there, but the satisfaction when each of these projects is finished is more than rewarding when the process is yours.

So before questioning whether something is going to go viral, I think it's more important to ask yourself a different question:

If nobody saw it, would it still be worth doing?

If the answer is no, maybe you're building something too dependent on other people's reaction. **Something fragile.**

Making things for yourself doesn't guarantee success, but it does hugely raise the odds of lasting long enough in time for a real opportunity to come along.
